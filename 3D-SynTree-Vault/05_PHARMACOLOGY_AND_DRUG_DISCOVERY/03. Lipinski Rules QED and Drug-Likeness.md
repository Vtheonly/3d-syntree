# 03. Lipinski Rules QED and Drug-Likeness

### 1. What It Is

Drug-likeness refers to the structural and physicochemical properties that make a small molecule likely to succeed as an orally administered drug in humans. The most widely used framework is **Lipinski's Rule of 5 (Ro5)**, which predicts poor absorption or permeation when:

- Molecular weight > 500 Da
- Calculated log P (cLogP) > 5
- Hydrogen bond donors (HBD) > 5
- Hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA) > 10

**Veber's rules** add:
- Rotatable bonds ≤ 10
- Topological polar surface area (TPSA) ≤ 140 Å²

**QED (Quantitative Estimate of Drug-likeness)** combines multiple parameters into a continuous score from 0.0 (undesirable) to 1.0 (favorable), using desirability functions for each property.

### 2. Why It Exists

Many potent binders fail as drugs because they cannot be absorbed, distributed, or formulated. A molecule that binds tightly to its target but cannot cross the intestinal wall, is insoluble, or is toxic is useless as a medicine.

The Rule of 5 emerged from analyzing thousands of orally administered drugs and finding that most fall within these boundaries. It is not a law—many excellent drugs violate one or more rules—but it is a useful filter for prioritizing compounds.

For 3D-SynTree, drug-likeness prevents the model from gaming the reward function by building massive, lipophilic molecules that fill every pocket groove but are completely undevelopable.

### 3. How It Works

#### Lipinski's Rule of 5

| Property | Threshold | Rationale |
|----------|-----------|-----------|
| Molecular weight | ≤ 500 Da | Larger molecules diffuse poorly across membranes |
| cLogP | ≤ 5 | Excessive lipophilicity causes poor solubility and non-specific binding |
| HBD | ≤ 5 | Too many donors hurt permeability and increase desolvation cost |
| HBA | ≤ 10 | Too many acceptors hurt permeability and increase desolvation cost |

"Rule of 5" because all thresholds are multiples of 5.

#### Veber's Rules

| Property | Threshold | Rationale |
|----------|-----------|-----------|
| Rotatable bonds | ≤ 10 | Flexible molecules pay entropic penalty upon binding |
| TPSA | ≤ 140 Å² | High polar surface area reduces membrane permeability |

#### QED Scoring

QED transforms each property into a desirability function (0 to 1) and combines them geometrically:

$$\text{QED} = \prod_i d_i(w_i)$$

where d_i is the desirability of property i and w_i is its weight. QED = 1.0 represents an ideal drug-like molecule; QED = 0.0 represents a poor one.

### 4. What It Consists Of

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Molecular weight (MW) | Sum of atomic masses (Da) |
| cLogP | Calculated octanol-water partition coefficient |
| HBD | Hydrogen bond donor count |
| HBA | Hydrogen bond acceptor count |
| TPSA | Topological polar surface area (Å²) |
| Rotatable bonds | Number of freely rotating single bonds |
| QED | Composite drug-likeness score (0–1) |
| Ro5 violation | Count of Lipinski rule violations |
| Veber compliance | Meeting rotatable bond and TPSA thresholds |

### 5. What You Must Understand

- **Ro5 is a filter, not a disqualifier.** Many blockbuster drugs (e.g., atorvastatin, cyclosporine) violate one or more rules. The rule identifies likely problems, not impossible ones.

- **QED is continuous, Ro5 is binary.** QED gives a nuanced score (e.g., 0.72 vs 0.45) rather than just pass/fail. This makes it better for reward functions where you want smooth gradients.

- **Generative models can cheat rewards.** A model can build a 1000-Da molecule with perfect shape complementarity that scores well on docking but is impossible to formulate. Drug-likeness terms in the reward prevent this.

- **Common pitfall:** Optimizing QED alone. A molecule with QED = 0.9 but no affinity for the target is useless. QED must be balanced against binding affinity.

- **Common pitfall:** Ignoring that cLogP is calculated, not measured. Different calculators (Crippen, XlogP, etc.) give different values. QED uses a specific implementation.
