# 04. Chemoselectivity and Reactivity Tiers

### 1. What It Is

Chemoselectivity is the preference of a chemical reagent to react at one specific functional group over competing groups on the same molecule. When a molecule carries multiple potential reactive sites (e.g., an amine, an alcohol, and a carboxylic acid), not all of them will react under a given set of conditions. Chemoselectivity determines which site wins.

**Reactivity tiers** are a hierarchical ranking of functional groups by their intrinsic reactivity toward a particular transformation. 3D-SynTree assigns every exposed handle on a molecule to a tier, and when multiple handles could potentially react, the policy directs incoming synthons to the highest-priority (most reactive) handle.

### 2. Why It Exists

Real molecules are rarely monofunctional. A drug-like molecule may contain amines, alcohols, carboxylic acids, halides, and aromatic rings simultaneously. If a synthesis reagent reacted indiscriminately at every available site, the result would be a complex mixture of undesired products—cross-reactions, oligomerization, and polymer formation.

For 3D-SynTree, chemoselectivity is essential because:

- **The action space must be deterministic.** Given a state (pocket + intermediate ligand), the set of legal reactions must be well-defined, not a probabilistic soup of competing products.
- **Self-reaction must be prevented.** A bifunctional building block with two amine handles could react with itself, forming polymers. Reactivity tiering prevents this by directing reactions to chemically favored sites.
- **Regioselectivity is enforced.** When a molecule has two similar handles, the model must know which one is more likely to react.

### 3. How It Works

#### Reactivity Hierarchy

Nucleophiles and electrophiles follow well-established reactivity orders. For amide coupling (the most common reaction in 3D-SynTree):

| Tier | Functional Group | Relative Reactivity | Example |
|------|-----------------|-------------------|---------|
| 1 | Primary aliphatic amine | ~10⁶ × baseline | –CH₂NH₂ |
| 2 | Aromatic amine / aniline | Baseline | Ar–NH₂ |
| 3 | Carboxylic acid / aldehyde | Lower | –COOH, –CHO |
| 4 | Alcohol | Much lower | –OH |
| 5 | Aryl halide | Lowest (for nucleophilic substitution) | Ar–Br |

A primary aliphatic amine is approximately 10⁶ times more reactive in amide coupling than an aromatic aniline because:
- The aliphatic amine's nitrogen lone pair is fully available for nucleophilic attack
- The aniline's lone pair is delocalized into the aromatic ring, reducing its nucleophilicity
- The anilinium ion (protonated aniline) is even less reactive at pH 7.4

#### Tier Assignment in 3D-SynTree

The engine assigns reactivity tiers using `HANDLE_REACTIVITY_TIERS` in `syntree/chemistry/reactions.py`. The `rank_handles()` function sorts exposed handles by tier, and the policy's masking logic ensures that synthons are directed to the highest-tier available handle.

When multiple handles of the same tier exist, additional factors (steric accessibility, proximity to pocket hotspots, electronic environment) may break ties.

### 4. What It Consists Of

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Chemoselectivity | Preference for reaction at one functional group over others |
| Reactivity tier | Ranked category of functional group reactivity |
| rank_handles() | Function sorting handles by tier |
| HANDLE_REACTIVITY_TIERS | Dictionary mapping handle types to tier numbers |
| Competing reaction | Two or more handles that could react with the same reagent |
| Regioselectivity | Preference for one position over another on the same molecule |
| Protecting group | Temporary mask on a reactive handle to prevent unwanted reaction |
| Cross-reactivity | Unwanted reaction between two handles on the same molecule |

### 5. What You Must Understand

- **Reactivity is context-dependent.** Tier rankings assume standard conditions. A Tier 4 alcohol can outreact a Tier 1 amine if the amine is sterically hindered or protonated (at pH below its pKa).

- **3D-SynTree sorts handles by tier before synthon selection.** The policy does not randomly choose among exposed handles. It considers the highest-tier handle first, reducing the action space to chemically meaningful options.

- **Self-reaction is the primary failure mode.** A bifunctional synthon with two complementary handles could polymerize. Tier masking prevents this by ensuring only one handle on each molecule is considered "active" at a time.

- **Common pitfall:** Assuming all amines are equally reactive. An aniline (aromatic amine) is orders of magnitude less nucleophilic than benzylamine (aliphatic amine), despite both being "primary amines."

- **Common pitfall:** Forgetting that pH affects reactivity. At pH 7.4, aliphatic amines are mostly protonated (–NH₃⁺), which reduces their nucleophilicity. The reactivity tier system accounts for this implicitly through the physiological ionization model.

### 7. Project Connection

The chemoselectivity concepts map directly to the 3D-SynTree codebase:

- **`HANDLE_REACTIVITY_TIERS`** in `syntree/chemistry/reactions.py`: Dictionary mapping handle class names to tier integers (Tier 1 = aliphatic amines, Tier 2 = aromatic amines, etc.).

- **`rank_handles()`** in `syntree/chemistry/reactions.py`: Sorts a list of exposed handles by reactivity tier, returning them in priority order.

- **`Issue #40: [CHEM-06]`**: Implements filters for competing handles and cross-reactivity, preventing self-reaction and oligomerization during trajectory extraction.

- **Legal action masks** in `syntree/engine/environment.py`: The `legal_action_masks()` method uses tier information to mask out chemically invalid handle selections.
