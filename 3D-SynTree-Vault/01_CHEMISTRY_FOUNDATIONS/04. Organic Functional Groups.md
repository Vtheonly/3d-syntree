# 04. Organic Functional Groups

### 1. What It Is

An organic functional group is a specific arrangement of atoms within a molecule that gives the molecule characteristic chemical reactions, regardless of the size or complexity of the rest of the molecule. Functional groups are the chemically active "centers" of organic molecules— the parts where bonds break and form during chemical reactions.

Think of a molecule as having two parts: a structural framework (usually carbon and hydrogen atoms that provide shape and bulk) and functional groups (heteroatoms like oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, or halogens that provide reactivity). The framework determines the molecule's shape and physical properties; the functional groups determine its chemical behavior.

In drug-like molecules, functional groups are the points of interaction between the ligand and the protein binding pocket. A carboxylic acid group can form a salt bridge with a lysine residue. An amine can donate a hydrogen bond to a backbone carbonyl. A halogen can occupy a hydrophobic subpocket. Every functional group has a predictable personality—it behaves similarly whether it appears in a simple molecule or a complex drug.

### 2. Why It Exists

Functional groups exist because heteroatoms interrupt the symmetry of hydrocarbon chains. Carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen bonds are relatively unreactive and non-polar. When an oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, or halogen replaces a hydrogen or inserts into a carbon chain, it creates an electron-rich or electron-poor region—a reactive site.

This matters for 3D-SynTree in two ways:

1. **Synthesis:** Every bond the model forms joins two functional groups. A carboxylic acid reacts with an amine to form an amide. A sulfonyl chloride reacts with an amine to form a sulfonamide. Without functional groups, there would be no predictable way to assemble molecules from building blocks.
2. **Binding:** Protein binding pockets are lined with amino acid side chains that present specific functional groups (carboxylates on Asp/Glu, ammoniums on Lys/Arg, hydroxyls on Ser/Thr, imidazoles on His). A ligand's functional groups must complement these pocket features to bind tightly.

### 3. How It Works

Functional groups work through three mechanisms:

**Electronic effects:** Heteroatoms have different electronegativities than carbon. Oxygen (3.44) and nitrogen (3.04) pull electron density away from carbon (2.55), creating partial positive charges on carbon and partial negative charges on the heteroatom. This polarization makes the heteroatom a hydrogen bond acceptor and the adjacent carbon an electrophile.

**Steric effects:** The size and shape of a functional group determine whether it can fit into a particular pocket subpocket. A tert-butyl group is bulky and hydrophobic; a hydroxyl is small and polar.

**Hydrogen bonding:** Functional groups with O–H, N–H, or S–H bonds can donate hydrogen bonds. Functional groups with lone pairs on O, N, or S can accept hydrogen bonds. These directional interactions are the primary mechanism of molecular recognition in protein-ligand binding.

### 4. What It Consists Of

| Functional Group | Structure | Role in 3D-SynTree |
|-----------------|-----------|-------------------|
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | Acidic handle, forms amides/esters, ionized at pH 7.4 |
| Primary/secondary amine | –NH₂, –NHR | Basic handle, forms amides/sulfonamides/ureas, protonated at pH 7.4 |
| Amide | –CONH–, –CONR– | Resonance-locked, planar, hydrogen bond donor/acceptor |
| Sulfonyl chloride | –SO₂Cl | Electrophilic handle, forms sulfonamides with amines |
| Sulfonamide | –SO₂NH– | Stable, polar junction, common in FDA drugs |
| Aldehyde | –CHO | Electrophilic carbon, forms reductive aminations |
| Ketone | –COR | Electrophilic carbon, less reactive than aldehyde |
| Alcohol | –OH | Nucleophilic oxygen, forms esters/ethers, H-bond donor/acceptor |
| Ether | –O– | H-bond acceptor only, metabolically stable |
| Aryl halide | Ar–X | Electrophilic carbon, forms Suzuki/SNAr/Buchwald-Hartwig products |
| Boronic acid | –B(OH)₂ | Nucleophilic carbon partner in Suzuki coupling |
| Alkyne | –C≡CH | Click chemistry partner with azides |
| Azide | –N₃ | Click chemistry partner with alkynes |

**Reactive handle:** A specific atom within a functional group that participates in bond formation. For example, in a carboxylic acid, the carbonyl carbon is the reactive handle that gets attacked by an amine.

**Leaving group:** The fragment that departs during a reaction (e.g., –OH from carboxylic acid in amide coupling, Cl from sulfonyl chloride, Br from aryl halide).

### 5. What You Must Understand

- **Every action in 3D-SynTree pairs two compatible functional groups.** The policy does not choose random atoms to bond; it chooses a handle on the growing ligand and a compatible handle on an incoming synthon, then selects a reaction template that joins them. If no compatible pair exists, that synthesis path is masked out.

- **Functional group compatibility determines reaction outcome.** You cannot couple a carboxylic acid with another carboxylic acid using the amide template—you need an amine. The 32 certified reactions each define specific handle pairs that are chemically valid.

- **Not all instances of a functional group are equally reactive.** An aliphatic amine (pKa ≈ 10.5) is far more nucleophilic than an aniline (pKa ≈ 4.5) because the aniline's lone pair is delocalized into the aromatic ring. This is the basis of chemoselectivity (see [[02_MEDICINAL_CHEMISTRY_AND_SYNTHESIS/04_Chemoselectivity_and_Reactivity_Tiers]]).

- **Functional groups define hydrogen bonding patterns.** A molecule with many donors and acceptors will have different binding kinetics than a hydrophobic molecule of the same size. This is captured in QED, TPSA, and the key-interaction reward.

- **Common pitfall:** Students often treat functional groups as isolated labels rather than electronic environments. A carboxylic acid next to a nitro group is more acidic than one next to a methyl group because the nitro group withdraws electron density, stabilizing the carboxylate anion.

- **Common pitfall:** Amides and esters are not freely rotatable despite being formally single bonds. Resonance gives them partial double-bond character (see [[01_CHEMISTRY_FOUNDATIONS/05_Resistance_and_Conjugation|05. Resistance and Conjugation]]).

### 7. Project Connection

The functional group concepts map directly to the 3D-SynTree codebase:

- **HANDLE_SMARTS** in `syntree/chemistry/reactions.py`: Defines all 10 reactive handle classes as SMARTS patterns (carboxylic_acid, primary_secondary_amine, aryl_halide, boronic_acid, aldehyde, alcohol, alkyne, azide, sulfonyl_chloride, alkyl_halide). Each pattern identifies the reactive atom(s) on a molecule that can participate in a reaction.

- **handle_features** in `syntree/data/featurizer.py`: The 64-dimensional handle feature vector explicitly encodes functional group classifications (slots 52–63), allowing the policy to distinguish handle types when selecting reaction partners.

- **Reaction side pairing** (`REACTION_SIDES` in `syntree/chemistry/reactions.py`): Maps each reaction family to its two compatible handle sides (e.g., amide_coupling pairs carboxylic_acid with primary_secondary_amine).

- **Reaction head** (`syntree/models/reaction_head.py`): Predicts which reaction family to apply based on the chemistry of the exposed handles.
