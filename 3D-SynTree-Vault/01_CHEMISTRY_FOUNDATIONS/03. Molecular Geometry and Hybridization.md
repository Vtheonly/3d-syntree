# 03. Molecular Geometry and Hybridization

### 1. What It Is

Molecular geometry is the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms in a molecule. When atoms form chemical bonds, their atomic orbitals rearrange into new hybrid orbitals with specific orientations in space. This process is called **hybridization**. The geometry of a molecule—its bond angles, bond lengths, and overall shape—is determined by the hybridization of its atoms and the repulsion between electron pairs.

Hybridization explains why methane (CH₄) is tetrahedral with 109.5° bond angles, why ethylene (C₂H₄) is planar with 120° angles, and why acetylene (C₂H₂) is linear with 180° angles. These geometric constraints are not arbitrary; they arise from quantum mechanical principles governing electron behavior.

### 2. Why It Exists

Molecular geometry exists because electron pairs repel each other. According to **VSEPR theory** (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion), electron pairs around a central atom arrange themselves to minimize repulsion, maximizing the distance between them. This geometric optimization determines molecular shape.

In 3D-SynTree, molecular geometry is critical because:

- **$Fsp^3$** (the fraction of $sp^3$-hybridized carbons) is the central thesis parameter of the project, with a constraint of $Fsp^3 \ge 0.40$ to avoid flat, greasy molecules
- **$sp^2$ centers remain locked** during torsion prediction, while single bonds ($\sigma$ bonds between $sp^3$ centers) are the only bonds that can rotate
- **The torsion head** (`syntree/models/torsion_head.py`) acts only on single bonds, not on double bonds or aromatic systems
- **Amide planarity** (from $sp^2$ hybridization) constrains which bonds can rotate during 3D conformer generation

### 3. How It Works

#### Hybridization Theory

When an atom forms bonds, its atomic orbitals (s, p, d, f) mix to form **hybrid orbitals** with new energies and geometries:

| Hybridization | Orbital Mixing | Geometry | Bond Angles | Example |
|---------------|---------------|----------|-------------|---------|
| $sp^3$ | One s + three p | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | Methane (CH₄), ethane |
| $sp^2$ | One s + two p | Trigonal planar | 120° | Ethylene (C₂H₄), benzene, carbonyls |
| $sp$ | One s + one p | Linear | 180° | Acetylene (C₂H₂), nitriles |

#### $sp^3$ Hybridization (Tetrahedral)

Carbon with four single bonds is $sp^3$ hybridized. The four hybrid orbitals point toward the corners of a tetrahedron, with bond angles of approximately 109.5°. This creates a three-dimensional, non-planar shape.

Molecules rich in $sp^3$ carbons are **3D-saturated**—they occupy volume in space rather than lying flat. This is desirable in drug design because 3D molecules tend to have better solubility, better target selectivity, and improved clinical success rates.

#### $sp^2$ Hybridization (Trigonal Planar)

Carbon with one double bond (or part of an aromatic ring) is $sp^2$ hybridized. The three $sp^2$ orbitals lie in a plane at 120° angles, while the unhybridized $p$ orbital forms $\pi$ bonds above and below the plane.

$sp^2$ centers are **rigid and planar**. Double bonds and aromatic rings cannot rotate freely. This creates flat, extended molecular structures that can stack via $\pi$-$\pi$ interactions but may suffer from poor solubility and non-specific binding.

#### $sp$ Hybridization (Linear)

Carbon with a triple bond is $sp$ hybridized. The two $sp$ orbitals point in opposite directions (180°), creating a linear geometry. Triple bonds are rigid and linear.

#### $\sigma$ and $\pi$ Bonds

- **$\sigma$ (sigma) bonds:** Formed by head-on overlap of orbitals along the bond axis. Electron density is concentrated between the two nuclei. Single bonds are pure $\sigma$ bonds. $\sigma$ bonds around $sp^3$ centers can rotate (torsion).
- **$\pi$ (pi) bonds:** Formed by lateral (side-by-side) overlap of $p$ orbitals above and below the bond axis. Present in double and triple bonds. $\pi$ bonds create a barrier to rotation—rotating would break the lateral overlap.

### 4. What It Consists Of

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| $sp^3$ orbital | Hybrid orbital from one s + three p orbitals; tetrahedral |
| $sp^2$ orbital | Hybrid orbital from one s + two p orbitals; trigonal planar |
| $sp$ orbital | Hybrid orbital from one s + one p orbital; linear |
| $\sigma$ bond | Head-on orbital overlap; single bond; rotatable around axis |
| $\pi$ bond | Lateral $p$-orbital overlap; in double/triple bonds; rigid |
| Tetrahedral geometry | 109.5° bond angles; 3D shape |
| Trigonal planar geometry | 120° bond angles; flat shape |
| Linear geometry | 180° bond angles; rod-like shape |
| VSEPR theory | Electron pair repulsion determines geometry |
| Hybridization | Mixing of atomic orbitals to form hybrid orbitals |

### 5. What You Must Understand

- **You cannot freely bend bond angles.** Each hybridization state has an equilibrium bond angle determined by quantum mechanics. Deviating from this angle requires significant energy (angle strain). This is why cyclohexane chairs are stable but cyclopropane is highly strained.

- **Flat molecules are $sp^2$-dominated.** Aromatic rings, carbonyls, and alkenes are all $sp^2$ and planar. Molecules with many aromatic rings connected by single bonds (biaryls) tend to be flat overall, which leads to the "flat grease" problem—high lipophilicity, poor solubility, and non-specific binding.

- **3D drug molecules require high $sp^3$ fractions.** Saturated carbons introduce tetrahedral geometry, chiral centers, and non-planar shapes that disrupt crystal packing, improve aqueous solubility, and reduce off-target binding. This is why 3D-SynTree enforces $Fsp^3 \ge 0.40$.

- **Only single bonds ($\sigma$) between $sp^3$ centers rotate freely.** Double bonds, aromatic bonds, and amide bonds are rotationally locked. When 3D-SynTree predicts a dihedral angle, it acts on a newly formed single bond between two fragments, not on internal bonds within a rigid fragment.

- **Common pitfall:** Students often assume all single bonds rotate equally. In reality, rotation can be hindered by steric bulk, and bonds adjacent to $sp^2$ systems may have partial double-bond character (e.g., in amides, esters).

- **Common pitfall:** Hybridization is not a physical process that "hands" orbitals to atoms; it is a mathematical description of the observed geometry. The orbitals exist as hybridized states because that geometry minimizes energy.

### 7. Project Connection

The hybridization and molecular geometry concepts map directly to the 3D-SynTree codebase:

- **The $Fsp^3$ constraint** (`FractionCSP3(mol)`) in `syntree/chemistry/catalog.py` and `syntree/data/curation.py`: The catalog filters building blocks to maintain $Fsp^3 \ge 0.42$ (slightly above the 0.40 thesis threshold), ensuring the synthon library favors 3D-saturated fragments over flat aromatic systems.

- **The torsion head** (`syntree/models/torsion_head.py`): Acts on single bonds ($\sigma$ bonds) only. $sp^2$ centers in aromatic rings and carbonyls remain locked during dihedral prediction. The head predicts the torsion angle around a newly formed single bond between a core scaffold and an incoming synthon.

- **Resonance-locked junction detection** in `syntree/chemistry/conformer.py`: The `is_conjugated_junction()` function identifies amide and ester junctions (where $sp^2$ hybridization creates partial double-bond character) and prevents free rotation at these bonds. The `apply_junction_torsion()` function overrides neural torsion predictions for conjugated junctions, snapping them to $0^\circ$ (cis) or $180^\circ$ (trans).

- **Distance geometry and ETKDG** in `syntree/chemistry/conformer.py`: The `AllChem.ETKDGv3()` algorithm respects hybridization-dependent bond lengths and angles when embedding 3D coordinates, ensuring $sp^2$ centers remain planar and $sp^3$ centers maintain tetrahedral geometry.
