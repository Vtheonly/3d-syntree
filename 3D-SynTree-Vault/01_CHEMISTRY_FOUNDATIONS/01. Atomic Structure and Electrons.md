# 01. Atomic Structure and Electrons

### 1. What It Is

An atom is the smallest unit of matter that retains the chemical identity of an element. Every atom consists of a dense, positively charged central **nucleus** surrounded by a cloud of negatively charged **electrons**. The nucleus itself contains two types of subatomic particles: **protons**, which carry a positive electrical charge (+1), and **neutrons**, which carry no electrical charge (neutral). Electrons occupy regions of space around the nucleus called **orbitals** or **energy levels**, where they exist as probability distributions rather than fixed points.

The identity of an element is determined entirely by the number of protons in its nucleus, called the **atomic number** (symbol: $Z$). All atoms of the same element have the same $Z$. For example, every carbon atom has $Z = 6$ (six protons), every oxygen atom has $Z = 8$, and every nitrogen atom has $Z = 7$. If the number of protons changes, the element changes.

The total number of protons plus neutrons is the **mass number** (symbol: $A$). Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons; these variants are called **isotopes**. Isotopes of an element have identical chemical behavior because chemistry is governed by electrons, which depend on proton count, but they differ in mass and some nuclear properties.

### 2. Why It Exists

Atomic structure explains all chemical behavior. The arrangement of electrons around the nucleus determines:

- How atoms bond with each other to form molecules
- The three-dimensional shape of molecules
- How molecules interact with proteins and other biological targets
- Why some elements are reactive and others are inert

Without understanding atomic structure, it is impossible to understand chemical bonding, molecular geometry, reactivity, or the physical basis of drug-target interactions. Every concept in the 3D-SynTree project—from SMILES strings to force fields to neural network featurization—ultimately traces back to the properties of atoms and their electrons.

### 3. How It Works

#### The Nucleus

The nucleus is extremely small compared to the overall atom (about 1/100,000 of the atomic diameter) but contains nearly all the atom's mass. Protons and neutrons are packed tightly together by the **strong nuclear force**, which overcomes the electrostatic repulsion between positively charged protons.

- **Proton ($p^+$):** Charge +1, mass ~1 atomic mass unit (amu)
- **Neutron ($n^0$):** Charge 0, mass ~1 amu
- **Electron ($e^-$):** Charge −1, mass ~1/1836 amu (negligible compared to protons and neutrons)

#### Electrons and Orbitals

Electrons do not orbit the nucleus like planets around the sun. Instead, they exist in **quantum mechanical orbitals**—three-dimensional regions of space where there is a high probability of finding an electron. Orbitals are organized into **shells** (principal energy levels, labeled $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$) and **subshells** (labeled $s, p, d, f$).

- **$s$ orbitals:** Spherical, can hold 2 electrons
- **$p$ orbitals:** Dumbbell-shaped, three orientations ($p_x, p_y, p_z$), can hold 6 electrons total
- **$d$ orbitals:** More complex shapes, can hold 10 electrons total

Electrons fill orbitals according to the **Aufbau principle** (lowest energy first), the **Pauli exclusion principle** (no two electrons can have identical quantum states), and **Hund's rule** (electrons fill degenerate orbitals singly before pairing).

#### Valence Electrons and the Octet Rule

The electrons in the outermost shell are called **valence electrons**. These are the electrons that participate in chemical bonding. Atoms tend to be most stable when their valence shell is full—typically 8 electrons for the main-group elements (the **octet rule**), or 2 electrons for hydrogen and helium (the **duet rule**). Atoms achieve stable valence configurations by gaining, losing, or sharing electrons, which leads to chemical bond formation.

#### Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same $Z$) with different numbers of neutrons (different $A$). For example:
- Carbon-12: 6 protons, 6 neutrons
- Carbon-13: 6 protons, 7 neutrons
- Carbon-14: 6 protons, 8 neutrons

Isotopes have nearly identical chemical behavior because their electron configurations are the same. However, they differ in mass, which can be exploited for tracking atoms through chemical reactions (isotope labeling).

### 4. What It Consists Of

| Component | Symbol | Charge | Relative Mass | Location |
|-----------|--------|--------|---------------|----------|
| Proton | $p^+$ | +1 | ~1 amu | Nucleus |
| Neutron | $n^0$ | 0 | ~1 amu | Nucleus |
| Electron | $e^-$ | −1 | ~1/1836 amu | Electron cloud (orbitals) |

**Key terms:**
- **Atomic number ($Z$):** Number of protons; defines elemental identity
- **Mass number ($A$):** Protons + neutrons
- **Isotope:** Same element, different neutron count
- **Valence shell:** Outermost electron shell
- **Valence electrons:** Electrons in the outermost shell
- **Orbital:** Probability region for electron location
- **Octet rule:** Tendency for atoms to seek 8 valence electrons

### 5. What You Must Understand

- **Atomic number determines chemical identity.** Two atoms with different proton counts are different elements, regardless of neutron count or electron arrangement.
- **Isotopes share chemistry but differ in mass.** This is critically important in 3D-SynTree, where isotope tags are used to track atom lineage through chemical reactions without changing chemical behavior.
- **Electrons drive chemistry.** The nucleus defines *what* an element is, but the electrons determine *how* it behaves chemically. Valence electrons are responsible for all bonding and reactivity.
- **The octet rule is a heuristic, not a law.** It works well for main-group elements (C, N, O, F) but has exceptions (e.g., boron, transition metals). In drug-like molecules, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen generally obey it.
- **Common pitfall:** Do not confuse mass number with atomic mass. Atomic mass is the weighted average of all naturally occurring isotopes, while mass number is specific to one isotope.

### 7. Project Connection

The atomic structure concepts from this note map directly to several components of the 3D-SynTree codebase:

- **`pocket_z` and `ligand_z` tensors** in `syntree/data/featurizer.py`: These tensors store atomic numbers $Z \in \{1 \dots 100\}$ for every atom in the protein pocket and the growing ligand. Each atomic number is embedded via `self.atomic_embedding(Z)$, creating a learnable vector representation of each element type. The model uses these embeddings to distinguish carbon atoms from nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur, and other elements during message passing.

- **RDKit isotope tagging** in `syntree/chemistry/reactions.py`: The constants `_CORE_MAP_OFFSET = 1000` and `_SYNTHON_MAP_OFFSET = 2000` implement isotope provenance tracking. Before executing a chemical reaction, core scaffold atoms receive isotope labels of $1000 + i$ and incoming synthon atoms receive $2000 + j$. Because isotopes survive RDKit's reaction execution without altering chemical behavior, the product's atoms can be mapped back to their original 3D coordinates. This is essential for scaffold anchoring during 3D conformer generation.

- **Element embedding table** in `syntree/models/bipartite.py`: The `nn.Embedding(max_atomic_number, hidden_dim)` layer creates vector representations for elements up to atomic number 100, covering all biologically relevant elements (H, C, N, O, F, P, S, Cl, Br, I) and many others found in protein structures and chemical catalogs.
