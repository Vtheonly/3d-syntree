# 02. Chemical Bonds and Electronegativity

### 1. What It Is

A chemical bond is the electrostatic attraction that holds atoms together in molecules and compounds. When two or more atoms interact, their electron clouds and nuclei rearrange to form a lower-energy, more stable arrangement than the isolated atoms. This attraction is what we call a chemical bond.

Bonds form because isolated atoms have higher potential energy than bonded atoms with filled electron shells. By sharing or transferring electrons, atoms achieve more stable electron configurations (typically satisfying the octet rule), releasing energy in the process.

There are two fundamental types of chemical bonds:

1. **Covalent bonds:** Atoms share one or more pairs of electrons. Neither atom fully owns the shared electrons; instead, both nuclei attract the same electron pair.
2. **Ionic bonds:** One atom completely transfers one or more electrons to another atom, creating positively charged cations and negatively charged anions that attract each other electrostatically.

Most bonds in drug-like molecules and biological systems are covalent, with varying degrees of polarity depending on the atoms involved.

### 2. Why It Exists

Chemical bonds exist because the universe tends toward lower energy states. Isolated atoms with incomplete valence shells are energetically unstable. By forming bonds, atoms achieve electron configurations that resemble noble gases (filled shells), which minimizes their potential energy.

In the context of 3D-SynTree, chemical bonds determine:

- Which molecular fragments can be joined together during synthesis
- The three-dimensional geometry of the growing ligand
- How the ligand interacts with amino acid residues in a protein binding pocket
- The strength and directionality of intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, van der Waals contacts)

### 3. How It Works

#### Electronegativity and Bond Polarity

**Electronegativity** (symbol: $\chi$) is a measure of an atom's ability to attract shared electrons toward itself in a chemical bond. The most widely used scale is the Pauling scale, where fluorine is assigned the highest value (3.98) and cesium one of the lowest (0.79).

When two atoms form a covalent bond, the atom with higher electronegativity pulls the shared electron pair closer to itself. This creates an uneven distribution of electron density:

- The more electronegative atom develops a partial negative charge ($\delta^-$)
- The less electronegative atom develops a partial positive charge ($\delta^+$)

This separation of charge creates a **bond dipole moment**.

Key electronegativity values (Pauling scale) for biologically relevant elements:

| Element | Symbol | Electronegativity ($\chi$) |
|---------|--------|---------------------------|
| Fluorine | F | 3.98 |
| Oxygen | O | 3.44 |
| Chlorine | Cl | 3.16 |
| Nitrogen | N | 3.04 |
| Bromine | Br | 2.96 |
| Carbon | C | 2.55 |
| Sulfur | S | 2.58 |
| Hydrogen | H | 2.20 |
| Phosphorus | P | 2.19 |

#### Types of Covalent Bonds Based on Polarity

- **Non-polar covalent bond:** $\Delta\chi < 0.4$. Electrons are shared essentially equally. Example: C–C, C–H (nearly non-polar).
- **Polar covalent bond:** $0.4 \le \Delta\chi \le 1.7$. Electrons are shared unequally. Example: C–O ($\Delta\chi = 0.89$), C–N ($\Delta\chi = 0.49$), O–H ($\Delta\chi = 1.24$).
- **Ionic bond:** $\Delta\chi > 1.7$. Electron transfer is essentially complete. Example: Na–Cl ($\Delta\chi = 2.23$).

#### Bond Types by Bond Order

- **Single bond ($\sigma$ bond):** One shared electron pair. Formed by head-on overlap of orbitals. Longest and weakest of the covalent bond types; freely rotatable (with some steric constraints).
- **Double bond:** One $\sigma$ bond + one $\pi$ bond. The $\pi$ bond forms from lateral overlap of $p$ orbitals. Shorter and stronger than single bonds; rigid, prevents rotation around the bond axis.
- **Triple bond:** One $\sigma$ bond + two $\pi$ bonds. Shortest and strongest covalent bond; linear geometry around the bonded atoms.

### 4. What It Consists Of

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Covalent bond | Shared electron pair between atoms |
| Ionic bond | Electrostatic attraction between cations and anions |
| Electronegativity ($\chi$) | Atom's tendency to attract shared electrons |
| Bond dipole | Separation of charge within a bond ($\delta^+$, $\delta^-$) |
| Partial charge | Fractional charge on an atom due to bond polarity |
| $\sigma$ bond | Single bond from head-on orbital overlap |
| $\pi$ bond | Lateral overlap of $p$ orbitals in double/triple bonds |
| Bond order | Number of shared electron pairs (1, 2, or 3) |
| Bond length | Distance between two bonded nuclei |
| Bond dissociation energy | Energy required to break a bond |

### 5. What You Must Understand

- **Bonds are not rigid rods.** Despite common depictions, chemical bonds behave more like flexible springs. They can stretch, bend, and rotate (for single bonds) within energy constraints. This flexibility is essential for molecular conformation and binding.

- **Polarity creates directional interactions.** Polar bonds create partial charges that guide molecular orientation. In a protein binding pocket, the partial positive end of a ligand bond will orient toward partial negative regions of the pocket (e.g., carbonyl oxygens), and vice versa. This is fundamental to molecular recognition.

- **Carbon and hydrogen are nearly non-polar together.** C–H bonds have $\Delta\chi = 0.35$, making them essentially non-polar. This is why hydrocarbon regions of molecules are hydrophobic—they do not form strong interactions with water.

- **Oxygen and nitrogen are highly electronegative.** They pull electron density away from carbon and hydrogen, creating strong dipoles. This makes C–O and C–N bonds important sites for hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with proteins.

- **Common pitfall:** Students often treat bonds as static and unchanging. In reality, bonds vibrate (stretch and bend) constantly at room temperature, and single bonds rotate. The 3D-SynTree conformational model must account for this flexibility when predicting dihedral angles.

- **Common pitfall:** Electronegativity differences create *partial* charges, not full integer charges (except in true ionic compounds). A carbon atom in a C–O bond does not become +1; it becomes $\delta^+$, perhaps +0.2 to +0.4 depending on the computational method.

### 7. Project Connection

The chemical bonding and electronegativity concepts map to multiple components of the 3D-SynTree codebase:

- **Dipole calculation and Gasteiger partial charge computation** in `syntree/chemistry/synthon_encoder.py`: The `compute_pharm3d_descriptor()` function calculates Gasteiger partial charges using `rdPartialCharges.ComputeGasteigerCharges()`. These partial charges quantify the electron redistribution caused by electronegativity differences and are included in the pharm3D feature vector (electrostatic features, $\mathbb{R}^5$).

- **Hydrogen-bonding and dipole alignment** in `syntree/engine/rl.py`: The `ThreeDReward` class evaluates hydrogen bond and salt bridge formation between ligand synthons and pocket residues. The partial charge distributions derived from electronegativity differences determine which ligand atoms can act as hydrogen bond donors or acceptors.

- **Pocket charge assignment** in `syntree/data/featurizer.py`: The `assign_pocket_formal_charges()` function applies physiological ionization rules (Asp/Glu −1.0, Arg/Lys +1.0), which create full charges rather than partial charges. These full charges enable salt bridges—strong electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged atoms.

- **Bond type encoding** in the handle feature vector: The 64-dimensional handle feature vector in `syntree/data/featurizer.py` encodes bond and atom properties that derive from electronegativity and bond type classifications.

### 8. Required Depth

**Essential.** Bond polarity and electronegativity underlie partial charges, hydrogen bonding, salt bridges, and dipole interactions—all of which are central to protein-ligand binding and the 3D-SynTree reward function.
