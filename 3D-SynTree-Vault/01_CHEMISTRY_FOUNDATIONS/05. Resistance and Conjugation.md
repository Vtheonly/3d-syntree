# 05. Resistance and Conjugation

### 1. What It Is

Resonance is the delocalization of π-electrons across adjacent overlapping p-orbitals, creating a hybrid structure that is more stable than any single Lewis structure would suggest. A molecule with resonance does not flip back and forth between different structures; it exists as a single weighted average (resonance hybrid) of all valid contributing forms.

Conjugation occurs when p-orbitals are aligned across a continuous chain of atoms (typically alternating single and double bonds), allowing π-electrons to spread over multiple atoms. This creates extended electron clouds with unique chemical and physical properties.

The most important consequence for 3D-SynTree is the **amide planarity lock**: the C–N bond in an amide has partial double-bond character (bond order ≈ 1.4) because the nitrogen lone pair delocalizes into the carbonyl π* orbital. This creates an 18–22 kcal/mol barrier to rotation, locking the amide into a planar geometry.

### 2. Why It Exists

Resonance exists because electron delocalization lowers a molecule's internal energy. When electrons are spread over more atoms, the electron-electron repulsion decreases and the system becomes more stable. Nature favors arrangements that maximize orbital overlap and minimize energy.

In drug discovery and 3D-SynTree, resonance is critical because:

- **Amide bonds are planar and rigid**, which constrains the 3D shape of peptides and many drug molecules
- **You cannot freely rotate amide or ester bonds** without introducing extreme strain—doing so generates an unphysical high-energy structure
- **Conjugated systems are flat**, contributing to the "flat grease" problem when overused
- **Resonance affects reactivity**, making amide carbonyls less electrophilic than ketone carbonyls

### 3. How It Works

#### Amide Resonance

In an amide (R–C(=O)–N–R'), the nitrogen atom is nominally sp³ hybridized with a lone pair. However, this lone pair can overlap with the carbonyl carbon's empty p-orbital (part of the C=O π bond). The result is two major resonance contributors:

1. **Major contributor:** R–C(=O)–N–R' (neutral, C=O double bond, C–N single bond)
2. **Minor contributor:** R–C(O⁻)=N⁺–R' (charged, C–O single bond, C=N double bond)

The true structure is a hybrid. The C–N bond has partial double-bond character (bond order ≈ 1.4), and the C–O bond has partial single-bond character. The amide group is planar: the carbonyl carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, and the atoms attached to nitrogen all lie in the same plane.

#### Rotational Barrier

Rotating around the C–N bond would destroy the p-orbital overlap, eliminating resonance stabilization. The energy cost is approximately 18–22 kcal/mol at room temperature—high enough that amides are effectively locked in place on biologically relevant timescales.

Two planar conformations exist:
- **Trans (180°):** The carbonyl oxygen and the N-substituent are on opposite sides (lower energy, predominant)
- **Cis (0°):** They are on the same side (higher energy, rare except in proline-containing peptides)

#### Ester Resonance

Esters (R–C(=O)–O–R') show similar behavior. The oxygen lone pair delocalizes into the carbonyl, giving the C–O bond partial double-bond character and creating a planar arrangement. The rotational barrier is slightly lower than amides (~15 kcal/mol) but still significant.

### 4. What It Consists Of

| Concept | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Resonance hybrid | The true structure, a weighted average of contributing forms |
| Contributing structures | Individual Lewis structures that participate in resonance |
| Partial double bond | A bond with bond order between 1 and 2 due to resonance |
| Bond order | Number of effective electron pairs shared (≈1.4 for amide C–N) |
| Resonance stabilization energy | Energy lowering from delocalization (~15–20 kcal/mol for amides) |
| Conjugated system | Chain of overlapping p-orbitals across alternating bonds |
| Planarity constraint | Geometric requirement for orbital overlap (atoms must be coplanar) |
| Rotational barrier | Energy required to rotate around a resonance-locked bond |
| Trans amide | 180° conformation, lower energy |
| Cis amide | 0° conformation, higher energy |

### 5. What You Must Understand

- **You cannot freely rotate an amide bond.** The 3D-SynTree torsion head acts only on true single bonds between sp³ centers. For amide and ester junctions, the `apply_junction_torsion()` function overrides any neural prediction and snaps the angle to 0° (cis) or 180° (trans), rotating an adjacent single bond instead.

- **Resonance changes reactivity.** An amide carbonyl is much less reactive toward nucleophiles than a ketone or aldehyde carbonyl because the resonance donation from nitrogen reduces the carbonyl carbon's electrophilicity.

- **Conjugated systems favor planarity.** Molecules with extended conjugation (polyenes, aromatic systems) tend to be flat. This contributes to high Fsp² and low Fsp³, which is associated with poor drug-like properties.

- **Common pitfall:** Students often draw amides with a pure C–N single bond and treat it like any other rotatable bond. This leads to physically impossible conformers in molecular modeling.

- **Common pitfall:** Resonance hybrids do not oscillate between structures. The molecule is a single, stable hybrid at all times.

### 7. Project Connection

The resonance and conjugation concepts map directly to the 3D-SynTree codebase:

- **`is_conjugated_junction()`** in `syntree/chemistry/conformer.py`: Identifies amide and ester junctions by detecting single bonds where one end carries a carbonyl (C=O) and the partner is N or O. Returns True for bonds that must not be freely rotated.

- **`apply_junction_torsion()`** in `syntree/chemistry/conformer.py`: Overrides neural torsion predictions for conjugated junctions, snapping them to 0° or 180° and redirecting torsion to an adjacent true single bond instead.

- **Torsion head masking** (`syntree/models/torsion_head.py`): The ContinuousTorsionHead does not predict angles for sp²–sp² conjugated bonds; those centers remain locked during conformer generation.
