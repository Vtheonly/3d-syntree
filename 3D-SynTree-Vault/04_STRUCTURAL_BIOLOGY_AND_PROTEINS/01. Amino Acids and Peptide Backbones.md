# 01. Amino Acids and Peptide Backbones

### 1. What It Is

Proteins are polymers built from 20 standard α-amino acid monomers connected head-to-tail by amide linkages called **peptide bonds**. Each amino acid shares a common backbone structure and is distinguished by its variable side chain (R group).

The generic amino acid structure is:

H₂N–CαH(R)–COOH

Where:
- Cα is the alpha carbon (central carbon)
- H₂N is the amino group (N-terminus direction)
- COOH is the carboxyl group (C-terminus direction)
- R is the variable side chain
- H is a hydrogen on Cα

The 20 standard amino acids are grouped by side chain chemistry:

| Category | Amino Acids | One-Letter Codes | Properties |
|----------|-------------|------------------|------------|
| Positively charged (basic) | Lysine, Arginine, Histidine | K, R, H | Protonated at pH 7.4, +1 charge |
| Negatively charged (acidic) | Aspartate, Glutamate | D, E | Deprotonated at pH 7.4, −1 charge |
| Aromatic | Phenylalanine, Tyrosine, Tryptophan | F, Y, W | Hydrophobic, π-stacking |
| Hydrophobic aliphatic | Leucine, Isoleucine, Valine, Alanine, Methionine | L, I, V, A, M | Non-polar, buried in core |
| Polar uncharged | Serine, Threonine, Asparagine, Glutamine | S, T, N, Q | H-bond donors/acceptors |
| Special | Glycine, Proline, Cysteine | G, P, C | G=flexible, P=rigid, C=disulfide |

### 2. Why It Exists

Amino acids are the fundamental building blocks of all proteins. The specific sequence and chemical nature of amino acids determine a protein's 3D structure and function. In drug discovery:

- **The amino acids lining a binding pocket determine what ligands can bind there.** A pocket lined with Asp and Glu (negative) will bind positively charged ligands; a pocket lined with Phe and Leu (hydrophobic) will bind lipophilic ligands.
- **Peptide bonds are planar and rigid** due to resonance (see [[01_CHEMISTRY_FOUNDATIONS/05_Resistance and Conjugation]]), constraining protein backbone conformations.
- **Side chain functional groups provide the chemical diversity** needed for catalysis, binding, and regulation.

### 3. How It Works

#### Peptide Bond Formation

Two amino acids join through a condensation reaction: the carboxyl group of one reacts with the amino group of the next, releasing water and forming a peptide bond (–CO–NH–).

The resulting chain has directionality:
- **N-terminus:** Free amino group (start)
- **C-terminus:** Free carboxyl group (end)

By convention, protein sequences are written N-to-C (left to right).

#### Backbone Torsion Angles

The polypeptide backbone has three key torsion angles:
- **φ (phi):** C–N–Cα–C (rotation around N–Cα bond)
- **ψ (psi):** N–Cα–C–N (rotation around Cα–C bond)
- **ω (omega):** Cα–C–N–Cα (peptide bond, nominally 180° trans)

The peptide bond has partial double-bond character (bond order ≈ 1.4) due to resonance, making ω strongly biased toward 180° (trans). This constrains the backbone to specific regions of conformational space.

### 4. What It Consists Of

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| α-carbon (Cα) | Central carbon of each amino acid |
| Peptide bond | Amide linkage (–CO–NH–) between amino acids |
| N-terminus | Free amino end of polypeptide |
| C-terminus | Free carboxyl end of polypeptide |
| Side chain (R) | Variable group distinguishing amino acids |
| φ (phi) | Backbone torsion N–Cα |
| ψ (psi) | Backbone torsion Cα–C |
| ω (omega) | Peptide bond torsion, ~180° trans |
| Disulfide bond | Covalent S–S link between cysteines |
| Zwitterion | Dipolar form with +NH₃ and −COO at pH 7.4 |

### 5. What You Must Understand

- **The chemical makeup of pocket-lining residues determines ligand compatibility.** A pocket rich in Arg/Lys (positive) will repel cationic ligands and attract anionic ones. A pocket rich in Phe/Leu/Val (hydrophobic) will favor lipophilic ligands.

- **All natural amino acids (except glycine) are chiral.** They have L-configuration (S at Cα, except cysteine which is R due to sulfur's higher atomic number). This creates a uniformly chiral protein surface.

- **Proline is special.** Its side chain loops back to the backbone nitrogen, creating a rigid cyclic structure that restricts φ angles and often appears in turns.

- **Glycine is special.** Its side chain is just H, giving it maximum conformational flexibility—it can adopt φ/ψ angles forbidden to other residues.

- **Common pitfall:** Treating amino acids as interchangeable. Each has distinct size, charge, polarity, and hydrogen-bonding capacity.

- **Common pitfall:** Forgetting that peptide bonds are planar. The backbone cannot twist freely at the C–N bond.

The amino acid concepts map directly to the 3D-SynTree codebase:
