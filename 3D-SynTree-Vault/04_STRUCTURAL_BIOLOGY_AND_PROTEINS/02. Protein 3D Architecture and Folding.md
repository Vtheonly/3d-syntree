# 02. Protein 3D Architecture and Folding

### 1. What It Is

Proteins have four levels of structural organization:

**Primary (1°) structure:** The linear amino acid sequence, held together by covalent peptide bonds.

**Secondary (2°) structure:** Local, regularly repeating structures stabilized by backbone hydrogen bonds. The two most common are:
- **α-helix:** Right-handed coil, 3.6 residues per turn, backbone N–H···O=C hydrogen bonds between residues i and i+4
- **β-sheet:** Extended strands connected by inter-strand hydrogen bonds, can be parallel or anti-parallel
- **Loops and turns:** Non-repetitive regions connecting secondary elements

**Tertiary (3°) structure:** The complete 3D folding of a single polypeptide chain into a globular shape. The hydrophobic core, side-chain packing, and specific interactions create a unique 3D architecture.

**Quaternary (4°) structure:** The spatial arrangement of multiple folded protein subunits (homomers or heteromers) in a complex.

### 2. Why It Exists

A linear amino acid sequence has no catalytic or binding activity by itself. It must fold into a specific 3D shape to create:
- **Binding pockets:** Concave clefts where ligands, substrates, or cofactors bind
- **Catalytic sites:** Precisely arranged residues that perform chemical transformations
- **Allosteric sites:** Regulatory binding sites distant from the active site

Protein folding is driven by the **hydrophobic effect**: non-polar side chains pack into the protein's interior, away from water. This is stabilized by:
- Interior hydrogen bonds
- Salt bridges (charged residue pairs)
- Disulfide bonds (Cys–S–S–Cys)
- van der Waals packing

### 3. How It Works

#### Secondary Structure Formation

**α-helix:** The backbone forms a right-handed spiral with 3.6 amino acids per turn. Each residue's backbone N–H donates a hydrogen bond to the carbonyl oxygen of the residue four positions ahead (i → i+4). Side chains point outward from the helix cylinder.

**β-sheet:** Two or more extended polypeptide strands align side-by-side. Backbone N–H and C=O groups form hydrogen bonds between strands. Adjacent strands can run in the same direction (parallel) or opposite directions (anti-parallel).

#### Tertiary Folding

The hydrophobic effect drives non-polar residues (Leu, Ile, Val, Phe, Ala, Met) to the protein interior. Polar residues (Ser, Thr, Asp, Glu, Lys, Arg) remain on the surface, interacting with water. Specific interactions (salt bridges, hydrogen bonds, disulfide bonds) lock the fold into a stable native state.

#### Binding Pocket Formation

Binding pockets are irregular 3D clefts formed by tertiary folding. Amino acids that are distant in the linear sequence (e.g., residues 25 and 187) can be brought into close spatial proximity by folding, creating a composite binding surface.

### 4. What It Consists Of

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Primary structure | Linear amino acid sequence |
| α-helix | Right-handed coil, 3.6 residues/turn, i→i+4 H-bonds |
| β-sheet | Extended strands with inter-strand H-bonds |
| Loops/turns | Non-repetitive connecting regions |
| Tertiary structure | Complete 3D fold of one chain |
| Quaternary structure | Multi-subunit complex arrangement |
| Hydrophobic core | Non-polar residues buried from water |
| Salt bridge | Charged residue pair interaction |
| Disulfide bond | Covalent Cys–S–S–Cys linkage |
| Binding pocket | Concave cleft for ligand binding |

### 5. What You Must Understand

- **Sequence does not directly reveal structure.** Two proteins with similar sequences fold similarly, but predicting 3D structure from sequence alone is the protein folding problem—difficult and computationally intensive.

- **Binding pockets are composite.** Residues far apart in sequence can be adjacent in 3D space. This means you cannot identify a pocket by looking at a local sequence window; you need the 3D structure.

- **Protein folding is spontaneous (Anfinsen's dogma).** The native structure corresponds to the global free energy minimum determined by the sequence. However, folding can be assisted by chaperones and can be kinetically trapped.

- **Common pitfall:** Assuming proteins are rigid. They have flexibility—side chains rotate, loops move, and some regions are intrinsically disordered.

- **Common pitfall:** Confusing secondary structure elements with domains. A domain is a larger, independently folding unit that may contain multiple helices and sheets.

### 7. Project Connection

The protein architecture concepts map directly to the 3D-SynTree codebase:

- **`scripts/preprocess_multidataset.py`**: Ingests raw PDB protein structures for use in 3D-SynTree training and evaluation.

- **`BipartitePaiNN`** in `syntree/models/bipartite.py`: Processes the 3D radius graph over pocket atoms, capturing spatial interactions between residues that are close in 3D but distant in sequence.

- **Pocket extraction** (`syntree/data/curation.py`, `syntree/data/multidataset.py`): Extracts 10 Å binding pockets from full protein structures, capturing the tertiary structure around the binding site.
